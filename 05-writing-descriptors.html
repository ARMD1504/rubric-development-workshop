Writing Descriptors That Markers Can Use

Most rubrics fail not because the criteria are wrong, but because the descriptors are useless. This page focuses on the skill that makes or breaks a rubric: writing descriptors that different markers will interpret the same way.

The Language Problem

The single biggest threat to rubric reliability is vague descriptor language (Dawson, 2017). When descriptors use subjective evaluative terms, different markers interpret them differently, and the rubric fails its most basic purpose: consistent scoring. Below are the four most common problems in descriptor writing (Brookhart, 2013).

1. Vague evaluative language

Words like “adequate,” “reasonable,” “somewhat,” and “fairly good” are evaluative judgements disguised as descriptions. What one marker considers “adequate,” another may consider “good.” These terms provide no guidance about what evidence to look for.

2. Shifting dimensions

The descriptor changes what it is measuring between levels. For example, a Level 4 descriptor might address analytical depth, while the Level 2 descriptor shifts to effort or length. This makes it impossible to compare performance across levels because the criterion itself is moving.

3. Deficit-only lowest levels

Describing the lowest level only by what is absent (“does not attempt,” “no evidence of”) tells markers nothing about what partial achievement looks like. Even at the lowest level, the descriptor should describe what is present in the work (Andrade, 2007).

4. Checklist disguised as descriptor

Listing features without describing quality turns a descriptor into a checklist. “Includes introduction, body, and conclusion” is a checklist. A descriptor should indicate how well those features are executed — for example, “Introduction clearly frames the argument and previews the structure of the analysis.”

Before and After

The following comparisons show how vague, non-parallel descriptors can be revised into specific, usable ones.

Pair 1: Vague vs. Observable

Before

“Shows good understanding of the topic.”

Problem: What does “good” mean? What evidence of understanding would a marker look for? Two markers could score the same work very differently using this descriptor.

After

“Identifies key concepts accurately and explains their relationships using discipline-specific terminology.”

Improvement: Observable — a marker can check for identification, accuracy, relationships, and use of terminology. The descriptor describes what the work looks like, not how the marker feels about it.

Pair 2: Non-parallel vs. Parallel

Before

Level 4: “Thorough analysis with excellent critical thinking.”

Level 2: “Some analysis with limited critical thinking.”

Problem: “Thorough” vs. “some” are vague modifiers. “Excellent” vs. “limited” do not specify what actually differs between levels. Both descriptors are evaluative labels, not descriptions.

After

Level 4: “Identifies 4+ relevant factors, evaluates their interactions, and supports conclusions with evidence from 3+ sources.”

Level 2: “Identifies 2–3 relevant factors with limited evaluation of their interactions and minimal supporting evidence.”

Improvement: Parallel — same dimension (factor identification, interaction evaluation, evidence use), changing only the degree. A marker can count factors and sources, making scoring more consistent.

Pair 3: Deficit-only vs. Descriptive

Before

Level 1: “No effort shown. Does not attempt the task.”

Problem: This describes absence, not presence. It measures effort rather than learning, and tells the marker nothing about what partial achievement looks like at this level.

After

Level 1: “Response is present but does not address the task requirements or addresses an unrelated topic.”

Improvement: Describes what IS present (a response exists) and specifies the nature of the shortcoming (does not address requirements). This gives the marker something concrete to look for.

A Practical Test

After writing your descriptors, you need to verify that they actually work. The most effective way to do this is a colleague calibration test (Dawson, 2017).

The Colleague Calibration Test

  1. Give the rubric to a colleague who was not involved in writing it. Do not explain the criteria — the rubric should speak for itself.
  2. Ask them to score a sample piece of student work using only the rubric. Choose a piece of work that sits near a level boundary.
  3. Compare their scores with yours. Score the same piece of work independently first.
  4. Where scores diverge, the descriptors need revision. Discuss with your colleague which descriptor was unclear or open to multiple interpretations, then rewrite it.

If two trained academics cannot agree on a score using your rubric, the problem is the rubric, not the markers.

Check Your Understanding

Question 1

Which descriptor is most useful for consistent marking?

  • a) “Demonstrates adequate understanding of key concepts”
  • b) “Shows good knowledge of the subject matter”
  • c) “Identifies at least 3 relevant concepts and explains how they apply to the given scenario”
  • d) “Has a reasonable grasp of the important ideas”
Option c is observable and specific — a marker can count concepts and check for application to the scenario. The other options use vague evaluative language (“adequate,” “good,” “reasonable”) that different markers will interpret differently, undermining scoring reliability.

Question 2

A rubric describes the lowest level as “Student did not submit or submitted work that is completely off-topic.” What is the problem?

  • a) The criterion is not valid
  • b) The descriptor only describes absence, not what is present at that level
  • c) The descriptor is not continuous
  • d) The weightage is wrong
Deficit-only descriptions of the lowest level tell markers nothing about partial achievement. Even at the lowest level, describe what IS present rather than only what is missing. A student who submits a partially relevant response deserves a descriptor that captures what that partial achievement looks like, not one that equates them with a non-submission.
Key Principle: A good descriptor answers the question: “What would I actually see in the student’s work at this level?” If you cannot picture the work from the descriptor alone, the descriptor needs revision. Descriptors should describe observable features of the work, not the marker’s subjective impression of it (Andrade, 2007).
