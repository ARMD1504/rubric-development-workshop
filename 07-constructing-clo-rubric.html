Constructing a CLO Rubric

On the last page, you saw how CLO rubrics differ from assessment rubrics. Now it is time to build one. The process will feel familiar — you are still defining criteria, setting levels, and writing descriptors — but there are two critical additions: mapping assessments to the CLO, and setting attainment thresholds that trigger CQI actions.

Steps to Construct a CLO Rubric

Building a CLO rubric follows six steps. Some overlap with what you did for assessment rubrics, but each step here is oriented toward a different question: not “how do I mark this task?” but “how do I know whether the outcome was achieved?” (Suskie, 2018).

  1. Start with the CLO statement and its cognitive level. Identify the Bloom’s level embedded in the CLO. A CLO at C4 (Analyse) needs criteria that measure analytical ability, not recall. The verb in the CLO statement is your anchor — it tells you what kind of cognitive evidence you need to collect (Biggs & Tang, 2011).
  2. Identify which assessments contribute to this CLO. Map the assessment tasks to the CLO. Not every task assesses every CLO. Use your course mapping matrix to determine which tasks provide evidence for this particular outcome (MQA, 2017).
  3. Determine the weight of each assessment’s contribution. A task weighted 40% contributes more evidence than one weighted 15%. The weighting reflects how much of the CLO’s evidence comes from each task, and it directly affects how the aggregated score is calculated.
  4. Define what “achieved” means at the outcome level. Set the threshold: for example, “CLO is attained if 60% of students score at Good (60) or above on the combined weighted assessment.” This threshold must be decided before the course begins, not retroactively adjusted after results come in (Banta & Palomba, 2014).
  5. Create the rubric grid. Build criteria from the CLO statement, set attainment levels from “Not Attained” to “Fully Attained,” and write descriptors that describe what attainment looks like at each level. The descriptors should focus on the proportion of students meeting the benchmark, not individual student performance.
  6. Set action triggers. What happens if the CLO is not attained? Define the CQI actions that will be initiated. This feeds directly into your programme’s continual quality improvement process and ensures that assessment data leads to tangible improvements (Kuh et al., 2015).

Worked Example

📋 Building a CLO Rubric: Data Analytics Course

CLO: “Apply data analytics techniques to solve business decision-making problems” (C3 Apply)

Assessments contributing to this CLO:

  • Lab Assignment (25%)
  • Midterm Project (35%)
  • Final Report (40%)

The CLO rubric below defines attainment levels based on the proportion of students who achieve a combined weighted score at or above the benchmark:

CLO Attainment Level Descriptor Threshold
Not Attained Less than 40% of students achieve a combined score of 50 or above Below 40%
Partially Attained 40–59% of students achieve a combined score of 50 or above 40–59%
Attained 60–79% of students achieve a combined score of 50 or above 60–79%
Fully Attained 80% or more students achieve a combined score of 50 or above 80%+

CQI action trigger: If attainment is below 60%, the CQI action might be: revise teaching methods for the analytics techniques, provide additional practice opportunities, or review the assessment design.

Notice the order: you set the threshold (Step 4) before you build the grid (Step 5). This is deliberate — deciding what “achieved” means before you see the data prevents retroactive adjustment and keeps the rubric honest as a quality assurance tool.

Setting Thresholds

How high should the bar be? That is what threshold-setting is about, and the honest answer is that it varies by institution and programme. Some programmes use 50% as the pass mark, others set it at 60% or even 70%. What matters most is consistency within your programme and alignment with institutional policy (Suskie, 2018). When thresholds are inconsistent across courses, it becomes impossible to compare attainment data meaningfully at the programme level.

Suskie (2018) recommended benchmarking your thresholds against comparable programmes and revisiting them periodically based on outcome data. A threshold that was appropriate five years ago may need adjustment if student demographics, teaching methods, or assessment designs have changed. But here is the important caveat: thresholds should never be lowered simply to make attainment data look better. That defeats the entire purpose of the CLO rubric as a quality assurance tool (Banta & Palomba, 2014).

When setting your threshold, ask yourself:

  • What is the institutional minimum passing grade?
  • What threshold do similar programmes in your discipline use?
  • Is the threshold appropriate for the cognitive level of the CLO?
  • Has the threshold been reviewed and approved by the programme committee?

Common Mistakes

⚠ Avoid These Pitfalls

  • Setting the threshold too low (e.g., anything above 0% = attained) — this masks real learning gaps and gives a false impression of success. If virtually every cohort “attains” every CLO, the rubric is not functioning as a quality assurance tool.
  • Using a single assessment to measure a CLO — triangulation from multiple sources is more reliable. A single task may be affected by task-specific factors (unclear instructions, time pressure, topic bias) that do not reflect actual learning (Kuh et al., 2015).
  • Not linking CQI actions to the rubric results — the rubric should drive improvement, not just generate data. If low attainment does not trigger a review of teaching methods, assessment design, or curriculum alignment, the CLO rubric becomes a bureaucratic exercise rather than a quality enhancement tool (MQA, 2017).

💡 Remember

Your CLO rubric is a quality assurance tool, not a grading tool. It tells you whether your course design is working, not whether individual students pass or fail.

Check Your Understanding

Question 1

A CLO rubric shows that only 35% of students achieved the CLO at the “Attained” level. The threshold was set at 60%. What is the most appropriate next step?

  • Lower the threshold so more students meet it
  • Initiate a CQI review of the teaching methods, assessment design, and rubric alignment for this CLO
  • Replace the CLO with an easier one
  • Add more assessment tasks to increase the sample
When attainment falls below the threshold, the appropriate response is a CQI review. Lowering the threshold without investigating the cause would mask the underlying problem.

Put the Steps in Order

Drag the steps of CLO rubric construction into the correct sequence by assigning a number (1 = first, 6 = last) to each step.

  • Create the rubric grid with criteria, attainment levels, and descriptors
  • Start with the CLO statement and identify its cognitive level
  • Define what “achieved” means at the outcome level (set the threshold)
  • Set CQI action triggers for when attainment falls below threshold
  • Identify which assessments contribute to this CLO
  • Determine the weight of each assessment’s contribution

Pause and Reflect

You have now worked through both assessment rubrics and CLO rubrics. Before you move on, take a moment to connect what you have read to your own context.

How does your institution currently measure CLO attainment? Does the process match what you have just read — aggregating across multiple tasks, setting thresholds before the course begins, and linking results to CQI actions? If not, what gaps do you notice?
