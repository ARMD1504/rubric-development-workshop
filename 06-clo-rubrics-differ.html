From Assessment Rubrics to CLO Rubrics

On Page 2, you learned about two structural types of rubrics: analytic (criteria scored separately) and holistic (overall judgment). Now we add a second dimension — the level at which the rubric operates. Up to this point, every rubric you have worked with has been for a single assessment task. But here is a question you have probably encountered in programme reviews: how do you know whether students actually achieved the CLO across the whole course, not just in one assignment? That requires a different kind of rubric.

What is a CLO Rubric?

A CLO rubric measures attainment of a learning outcome across multiple assessment tasks. Unlike an assessment rubric (which marks a single task), a CLO rubric aggregates evidence from multiple sources to determine whether the CLO has been achieved (Suskie, 2018). Think of it this way: an assessment rubric tells you how well a student did on one assignment. A CLO rubric tells you whether the intended learning actually happened across the whole course.

💡 Think of it this way

An assessment rubric answers “How well did the student perform on this task?”
A CLO rubric answers “Has the student achieved this learning outcome?”

This shift is not just administrative — it changes how you think about assessment. When you assess at the CLO level, you are no longer asking “how did this student do on this task?” You are asking something bigger: “did the intended learning actually occur across the breadth of the course?” That is a fundamentally different question, and it requires a fundamentally different tool (Biggs & Tang, 2011).

Key Differences

Here is a side-by-side comparison. Notice that the difference is not just about what gets scored — it is about who uses the data and why (Banta & Palomba, 2014).

📝

Assessment Rubric

  • Marks a single task
  • Criteria derived from task requirements
  • Produces a score for that task
  • Used by marker for grading
🎯

CLO Rubric

  • Measures outcome attainment across tasks
  • Criteria derived from the CLO statement
  • Produces an attainment percentage
  • Used for quality assurance and CQI

Two Dimensions, Four Possibilities

The structural choice (analytic vs. holistic) and the purpose choice (assessment vs. CLO) are independent decisions. This means there are four possible rubric types, depending on which combination suits your need:

Analytic Holistic
Assessment-level Analytic assessment rubric
Most common for coursework — criteria scored separately across levels		 Holistic assessment rubric
Quick overall judgment of a single task — useful for presentations or portfolios
CLO-level Analytic CLO rubric
Detailed outcome tracking across tasks — shows which aspects of the CLO are attained		 Holistic CLO rubric

In practice, most coursework assessment uses the top-left cell: analytic rubrics at the assessment level. This is what we focused on in Pages 3–5. For quality assurance and CQI purposes, CLO rubrics tend to be analytic as well, because programme coordinators need to see which dimensions of the outcome are being achieved and which are not. But holistic rubrics at either level have legitimate uses — for instance, a holistic assessment rubric for a final presentation where the overall impression is what matters, or a holistic CLO rubric when the outcome is best understood as an integrated competency rather than a set of separable skills.

These distinctions have practical implications. An assessment rubric tells a marker how to assign marks to a student’s essay, presentation, or project. A CLO rubric tells a programme coordinator whether the learning outcome has been met by the cohort as a whole (MQA, 2017).

The Mapping Chain

In outcome-based education, assessment data flows through a structured chain. Each level serves a distinct purpose, from task-level scoring to programme-level outcomes:

Assessment Task
→
Assessment Rubric
(scores the task)
→
CLO Rubric
(aggregates across tasks)
→
PLO Attainment

A single CLO might be assessed through 2–3 different tasks, each with its own assessment rubric. The CLO rubric brings these together to determine whether the CLO has been achieved at the course level. This aggregation process is what distinguishes outcome measurement from task marking (Suskie, 2018). It ensures that a student who performs well on one task but poorly on another is not mistakenly judged as having fully attained the outcome.

Why This Matters for Constructive Alignment

Remember the alignment chain from Page 1? CLO rubrics are what close the loop. They connect assessment results back to the intended outcomes, giving you evidence that alignment has worked — or revealing where it has not. Biggs and Tang (2011) made an important point: alignment must be verified, not merely designed. You can have a beautifully structured curriculum with aligned outcomes, activities, and assessments, but unless you confirm through evidence that students are actually achieving those outcomes, you are working on faith rather than data.

CLO rubrics provide that evidence. When attainment data shows that students are consistently meeting the CLO, it validates your design choices. When attainment is low, it signals a misalignment somewhere in the system — perhaps the teaching activities did not adequately prepare students, or the assessment tasks did not validly measure the outcome (Banta & Palomba, 2014). Either way, you now have something concrete to act on.

📋 Worked Example: CLO Rubric in Practice

CLO: “Evaluate business strategies using appropriate analytical frameworks” (C5 Evaluate)

This CLO is assessed through:

  • Task 1: Individual Case Analysis (30%) — assessment rubric scores framework application, analysis depth, evidence quality
  • Task 2: Group Strategy Report (40%) — assessment rubric scores evaluation quality, recommendation justification, teamwork
  • Task 3: Reflective Portfolio (30%) — assessment rubric scores critical reflection, self-assessment, professional development

The CLO rubric aggregates scores from all three tasks, weighting them according to their contribution to the CLO. Because the CLO operates at Bloom’s C5 (Evaluate), each assessment rubric must include criteria that genuinely measure evaluative thinking — not just recall or comprehension (MQA, 2017).

Question 2

A CLO at Bloom’s C5 (Evaluate) is assessed only through a multiple-choice test of definitions. The CLO rubric shows 90% attainment. What is the most likely problem?

  • The CLO rubric threshold is set too high
  • The assessment task does not measure evaluation ability, so the high attainment figure is misleading
  • The students are exceptionally well prepared
  • The rubric descriptors are not parallel
If the CLO requires evaluation (C5) but the task only tests recall (C1), high attainment scores do not mean students can actually evaluate. The task–rubric alignment is broken.

Key Point: CLO rubrics serve a dual purpose: they verify that your constructive alignment is working, and they provide the data needed for continual quality improvement (CQI).
