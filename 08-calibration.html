Calibration: Scoring Consistently

Even the best rubric produces inconsistent results if different markers interpret it differently. Calibration is the process of ensuring that all markers apply the rubric in the same way — and it starts with practising on sample work.

Why Calibration Matters

Inter-rater reliability refers to the degree to which different markers give the same score to the same piece of work. Without calibration, a student's grade depends partly on who marks their work, which is fundamentally unfair. Jonsson and Svingby (2007) emphasise that inter-rater reliability is a critical concern in rubric-based assessment, noting that even well-designed rubrics can yield inconsistent scoring when markers have not developed a shared understanding of the performance level descriptors.

Calibration addresses this problem directly. When markers calibrate, they discuss how they interpret the rubric, surface hidden assumptions, and reach consensus on what each performance level looks like in practice. This process not only improves scoring consistency but also frequently reveals areas where the rubric itself needs revision — ambiguous descriptors, missing criteria, or poorly defined cut scores (Rust et al., 2003).

Key Point: Calibration is not about forcing markers to agree — it is about building a shared interpretation of quality that is grounded in the rubric's language and the learning outcomes it measures.

How to Calibrate

The following process can be used by any marking team, whether you are coordinating a large course with multiple tutors or working with a small group of colleagues:

  1. All markers independently score the same sample using the rubric. Each marker records their score and a brief justification for each criterion.
  2. Compare scores and discuss differences. Go criterion by criterion. Where scores differ by more than one band, each marker explains their reasoning.
  3. Identify where the rubric language led to different interpretations. Ask: Which word or phrase caused the disagreement? Is the descriptor specific enough?
  4. Revise the rubric or agree on a shared interpretation. If the language is genuinely ambiguous, revise it. If the disagreement stems from different assumptions, document the agreed interpretation as a marking note.
  5. Repeat with a second sample until scores converge. Aim for agreement within one performance band across all markers before moving to live marking.

Literature coverage: Satisfactory (45–59)
The student mentions "some studies" but does not cite or synthesise specific sources. No authors, dates, or findings are referenced. The paragraph covers at most one or two vague allusions to research rather than identifiable sources, placing it in the "covers 1–2 sources with minimal organisation" band.
Critical analysis: Satisfactory to Fail (35–50)
The student acknowledges that "some studies show" benefits and "other studies say" it distracts, but does not actually analyse, compare, or evaluate any study. There is no engagement with methodology, no comparison of findings, and no identification of contradictions or gaps. The language is purely descriptive — the student reports that different views exist without examining them.
Argument structure: Satisfactory (45–59)
The paragraph has a direction ("will look at both sides of the argument") but no thesis statement. There is no claim being advanced and no evidence-based progression. The writing is descriptive rather than argumentative, though the stated intention to examine "both sides" gives it a minimal structural framework that prevents it from being entirely structureless.

Running Calibration in Your Department

Calibration works best as a structured, recurring activity rather than an ad hoc conversation. Here are practical tips for organising effective calibration sessions:

Tips for Organising Calibration Sessions

  • Select 3–4 sample works at different performance levels — include one strong, one mid-range, and one weak piece so markers can practise distinguishing across the full scale.
  • Allow approximately 30 minutes per sample — 10 minutes for independent scoring, 20 minutes for discussion and consensus-building.
  • Document agreed interpretations — create a shared marking guide that records any clarifications or decisions made during calibration. This becomes a reference for new markers and a record for quality assurance.
  • Schedule calibration before live marking begins — do not wait until discrepancies emerge in actual student grades.
  • Include new and experienced markers together — calibration is a powerful professional development tool for sessional and early-career academics (Sadler, 2010).
