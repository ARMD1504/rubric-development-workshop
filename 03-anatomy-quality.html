Anatomy of a Quality Rubric

Understanding the parts of a rubric is essential before you can build one. But knowing the anatomy is only half the battle — you also need to know what makes each part work well.

The Three Components Revisited

On Page 2, we introduced the three structural components of a rubric: criteria, descriptors, and the rating scale. Here, we go deeper into what makes each component function effectively in practice (Brookhart, 2013).

Criteria: Derive them from the task, not from intuition

Criteria should be derived directly from the assessment task and its associated Course Learning Outcome (CLO), not from habit or intuition. Each criterion should reflect what the CLO actually asks students to demonstrate. If a CLO is pitched at the “analyse” level of Bloom’s taxonomy, the criteria need to address analytical depth, not factual recall. A criterion such as “Recall of key definitions” would be misaligned with a CLO requiring analysis, even if it feels like a natural thing to assess (Wolf & Stevens, 2007).

Descriptors: Describe, do not label

Useful descriptors describe what the marker will observe in the student’s work. Labels such as “Excellent analysis” tell the marker nothing about what “excellent” looks like in practice. Compare that with: “Identifies 3+ relevant variables and explains their interactions with supporting evidence.” The second version tells the marker exactly what to look for and what counts as evidence of achievement (Moskal, 2000).

Rating scale: Choosing the right number of levels

In higher education, a rating scale of 4–6 levels is typical (Reddy & Andrade, 2010). An important design decision is whether to use an even or odd number of levels. An even-numbered scale (e.g., 4 levels) forces markers to make a decision — there is no midpoint to retreat to. An odd-numbered scale (e.g., 5 levels) provides a midpoint, which can be useful when performance genuinely sits between two categories.

Sunway University’s 6-Level Rating Scale

At Sunway, the institutional grading scale uses six levels:

  • Fail (0–39)
  • Pass (40–44)
  • Satisfactory (45–59)
  • Good (60–69)
  • Merit (70–79)
  • Distinction (80–100)

This 6-level even scale ensures that markers must commit to a judgement without defaulting to a midpoint.

Six Principles of Quality Rubrics

Research on rubric quality identifies six principles that distinguish well-designed rubrics from flawed ones (Brookhart, 2013; Wolf & Stevens, 2007). Use these as a checklist when reviewing any rubric.

  1. Continuous — There should be a smooth transition between performance levels, with no abrupt shifts in expectations. Each level builds logically on the one below it.
  2. Parallel — Each descriptor uses parallel language structure across levels. The dimension being assessed stays the same; only the degree of achievement changes.
  3. Coherent — The rubric stays focused on the same criteria across all levels. A descriptor at Level 1 should address the same criterion as the descriptor at Level 4, just at a lower degree of achievement.
  4. Valid — The rubric measures what it claims to measure and is aligned to the CLO. A criterion about “presentation” in a rubric assessing analytical ability would lack validity.
  5. Reliable — Language is descriptive enough for consistent scoring across different markers. Two markers using the same rubric on the same piece of work should arrive at similar scores.
  6. Suitable weightage — Criteria are weighted according to their relative importance to the CLO. The most important learning outcomes receive the highest weightage.

Case Study Activity: Spot the Problems

Case Study: A Flawed Rubric

Assessment task: Evaluate the effectiveness of a company’s digital marketing strategy.

CLO: C5 Evaluate — “Evaluate the effectiveness of digital marketing strategies in achieving organisational goals.”

Review the rubric below. It has 3 criteria with 4 performance levels. Can you identify the problems?

Criterion Level 4 (Excellent) Level 3 (Good) Level 2 (Satisfactory) Level 1 (Poor)
Content knowledge Demonstrates excellent knowledge of digital marketing concepts and strategies Demonstrates good knowledge of digital marketing concepts Shows some awareness of the topic Limited knowledge of the topic
Critical analysis Provides thorough critical analysis with multiple perspectives Provides adequate critical analysis with some perspectives Provides limited analysis No effort shown
Presentation and effort (highlight-problem) Report is well-formatted, visually appealing, and shows clear effort Report is generally well-formatted with some effort visible Report has formatting issues and appears hastily prepared Report is poorly formatted with no visible effort

Activity: Identify the Problems

Look at the rubric above and identify as many problems as you can. For each problem, consider which of the six quality principles it violates. Write down your observations before reading the answers below.

Hint: Look at the highlighted cells and think about what the CLO actually requires.

Problem 1: Not Parallel (Content Knowledge)
Level 4 says “Demonstrates excellent knowledge of digital marketing concepts and strategies” but Level 2 shifts to “Shows some awareness of the topic.” The descriptor abandons the dimension of knowledge and shifts to awareness — a different construct entirely. Parallel language requires that all levels describe the same dimension (knowledge of digital marketing) with changing degrees of achievement, not shifting to a different dimension partway down.
Problem 2: Not Coherent (Critical Analysis)
Level 4 describes “thorough critical analysis with multiple perspectives,” but Level 1 says “No effort shown.” This abandons the criterion entirely. “No effort” measures effort, not analytical ability. A coherent rubric keeps the same criterion in focus across all levels. Level 1 should describe what poor critical analysis looks like, not whether the student tried.
Problem 3: Not Valid (Presentation and Effort)
The CLO requires students to evaluate digital marketing strategies. A criterion about “presentation and effort” measures formatting quality and perceived effort — neither of which is related to evaluation ability. This criterion lacks validity because it does not measure what the CLO intends. It should be replaced with a criterion that directly addresses evaluation skill, such as “Quality of evaluative judgement and justification.”

Check Your Understanding

Question 1

A rubric descriptor at Level 3 says “The student provides a reasonable discussion.” What principle does this descriptor violate?

  • a) Continuity
  • b) Reliability — the language is too vague for consistent scoring
  • c) Suitable weightage
  • d) Coherence
“Reasonable discussion” is evaluative, not descriptive. Different markers will interpret “reasonable” differently, making scoring unreliable. A descriptor should specify what makes a discussion reasonable — for example, by indicating the number of points addressed or the type of evidence used.

Question 2

A CLO states “Demonstrate effective interpersonal communication skills.” The rubric criterion is “Written report follows APA formatting.” What quality issue is most significant?

  • a) The descriptor is not parallel
  • b) The rubric is not continuous
  • c) The criterion is not valid — it measures formatting, not interpersonal communication
  • d) The weightage is incorrect
Validity means the rubric measures what the CLO intends. APA formatting has nothing to do with interpersonal communication skills. The criterion does not align with the learning outcome, so even a perfect score on this criterion would tell us nothing about the student’s communication ability.

True or False? Quality Principles in Practice

1. A rubric descriptor at Level 4 says “Demonstrates excellent knowledge of digital marketing concepts” but at Level 2 shifts to “Shows some awareness of the topic.” This violates the principle of parallel language.
Correct. Parallel language requires that all levels describe the same dimension (knowledge of digital marketing) with changing degrees of achievement. Shifting from “knowledge of digital marketing concepts” to “awareness of the topic” abandons the original dimension and introduces a different construct entirely. The descriptor should stay focused on the same dimension at every level.
2. If two markers use the same rubric on the same student essay and arrive at very different scores, the rubric likely has a reliability problem.
Correct. Reliability means the rubric produces consistent scores across different markers. If two competent markers arrive at very different scores using the same rubric on the same work, the descriptors are likely too vague or subjective for consistent interpretation. More descriptive, observable language would improve inter-rater reliability.
3. A rubric that gives equal weight to all five criteria always satisfies the principle of suitable weightage.
Equal weighting only satisfies the principle if all criteria are genuinely equally important to the CLO. In most cases, some criteria are more central to the learning outcome than others. Suitable weightage means criteria are weighted according to their relative importance — the most important learning outcomes should receive the highest weightage, not an automatic equal share.
