Building an Assessment Rubric Step by Step

Theory becomes practice in this page. We will walk through the process of building an analytic rubric for a real higher-ed assessment, step by step.

The Process

Building a quality rubric is a systematic process. The following eight steps, drawn from Brookhart (2013) and Nitko and Brookhart (2011), provide a reliable sequence for developing an analytic rubric aligned to your CLO.

  1. Define what the task is measuring — Read the CLO and assessment brief carefully. What knowledge, skills, or attributes should the student demonstrate? Write a clear statement of what constitutes evidence of learning for this task.
  2. Draft 3–5 criteria aligned to the task — Each criterion should map to a specific aspect of what the CLO requires. Avoid criteria that overlap or measure the same thing. If two criteria always receive the same score, they may be redundant (Wolf & Stevens, 2007).
  3. Write the top-level descriptor first — Describe what “Distinction” or “Excellent” looks like for each criterion. Use concrete, observable language that tells the marker exactly what to look for in the student’s work.
  4. Scale down from the top level — Modify the top descriptor to create lower levels. Keep the language parallel — change the degree of achievement (e.g., number of factors, accuracy, depth), not the dimension being assessed.
  5. Set the cut scores — Define what constitutes a pass (minimal acceptable performance). Avoid deficit-only language at the lowest level. Even the lowest level should describe what is present, not only what is absent.
  6. Weight the criteria — Not all criteria are equal. Weight according to what the CLO prioritises. If the CLO emphasises analysis, the analysis criterion should carry the most weight.
  7. Test with sample work — Apply the rubric to 2–3 real or hypothetical student submissions. Note where the rubric fails to distinguish between levels or where markers disagree. This is the most important quality-assurance step.
  8. Revise and finalise — Based on testing, refine descriptors, adjust weightage, and ensure alignment. A rubric is a working document — expect to revise it after the first use.

Worked Example

Assessment: Group Case Study Report

Course: Business Strategy

CLO: “Analyse business problems using strategic management frameworks and recommend evidence-based solutions” (C4 Analyse)

Task: Students work in groups to analyse a real company’s strategic position and produce a report with recommendations.

Step 1: Define what the task is measuring

The CLO requires two things: (a) the ability to analyse using frameworks, and (b) the ability to recommend evidence-based solutions. The task measures analytical ability (using strategic frameworks), evidence-based reasoning, and solution quality.

Step 2: Draft 3–5 criteria

Based on the CLO, we derive four criteria:

  • Criterion (a): Application of strategic frameworks — 30%
  • Criterion (b): Quality of analysis and evidence use — 35%
  • Criterion (c): Feasibility and justification of recommendations — 25%
  • Criterion (d): Professional communication — 10%

Notice that the heaviest weight (35%) goes to analysis quality, which is the core of the CLO. Professional communication receives the lowest weight because it supports but is not central to the learning outcome.

Step 3: Write the top-level descriptor first

For each criterion, we describe what Distinction-level (80–100) work looks like:

Criterion (a) — Application of strategic frameworks (Distinction):
“Applies 2+ strategic frameworks accurately, integrating them to reveal complementary insights about the case. The selection of frameworks is justified and appropriate for the nature of the business problem.”
Criterion (b) — Quality of analysis and evidence use (Distinction):
“Analysis identifies 4+ relevant factors and evaluates their interactions systematically. Conclusions are supported by evidence from multiple credible sources, including academic literature and company data. The reasoning chain from evidence to conclusion is explicit and logical.”

Step 4: Scale down from the top level

Below is the full descriptor scale for Criterion (a): Application of strategic frameworks. Notice how the dimension (framework application) stays constant while the degree of achievement changes.

Level Score Range Descriptor: Application of Strategic Frameworks
Distinction 80–100 Applies 2+ strategic frameworks accurately, integrating them to reveal complementary insights about the case
Merit 70–79 Applies 2+ frameworks accurately with clear connections to the case
Good 60–69 Applies 1–2 frameworks with generally accurate application to the case
Satisfactory 45–59 Applies at least 1 framework with some inaccuracies in application
Pass 40–44 Attempts to apply a framework but with significant inaccuracies
Fail 0–39 Does not apply any recognisable strategic framework

Strategy Tip

Always write your top-level descriptor first. It is much easier to scale down from “excellent” than to build up from “poor.” When you start from the top, you establish the full standard of achievement and then describe what less of that achievement looks like. Starting from the bottom often leads to deficit-only language and vague descriptors (Brookhart, 2013).

Key Insight: Notice how the descriptor changes the degree of achievement (number of frameworks, accuracy, integration) while keeping the same dimension (framework application) constant across all levels. This is what “parallel” means. At every level, the marker is assessing the same thing — how well the student applies strategic frameworks — just at different degrees of proficiency.

Check Your Understanding

Question 1

When building a rubric, why is it recommended to write the top-level descriptor first?

  • a) Because top-level students are the most important
  • b) Because it establishes the full standard, making it easier to describe what less achievement looks like
  • c) Because the top level is the easiest to write
  • d) Because institutional policy requires it
Starting from the top establishes what full achievement looks like. You then modify the descriptor to describe progressively less achievement, maintaining parallel structure. This approach ensures that every level describes the same dimension at a different degree, rather than shifting what is being measured.

True or False?

1. When building a rubric, you should write the lowest-level descriptor first and then build up to the top level.
The recommended approach is the opposite: write the top-level descriptor first, then scale down. Starting from the top establishes what full achievement looks like, making it easier to describe what less of that achievement looks like at each lower level.
2. Testing a rubric with 2–3 sample student submissions is the most important quality-assurance step in rubric development.
Correct. Applying the rubric to real or hypothetical sample work reveals where descriptors are unclear, where markers disagree, and where the rubric fails to distinguish between performance levels. This calibration step is the single most effective way to improve a rubric before live use.
3. If two criteria in a rubric always receive the same score across multiple student submissions, they may be redundant.
Correct. When two criteria consistently produce identical scores, they are likely measuring the same underlying dimension. Wolf and Stevens (2007) recommend reviewing such criteria for overlap and either merging them or revising one so that it captures a genuinely distinct aspect of performance.
